3.4 Proposed Method 
3.4.1 Method Overview 
The proposed method detects sleep apnea by 
integrating ECG and respiratory signals in a multimodal deep 
learning pipeline. The pipeline has six stages: (1) multi-
dataset harmonization, (2) preprocessing, (3) reduced 
temporal windowing, (4) modality-specific Transformer 
encoding, (5) cross-attention fusion, and (6) pooling and 
window-level classification. Each window is classified as 
sleep apnea or normal. Unlike approaches that convert signals 
into time-frequency images before classification [41], [42], 
the proposed model operates directly on one-dimensional 
signal sequences, which keeps the pipeline simple and avoids 
a separate image-construction step. 
3.4.2  Multi Dataset Harmonization 
1. Channel mapping. ECG and respiratory channels are 
mapped to a unified naming scheme, so each recording 
provides an ECG channel and a respiratory channel. 
Respiratory sources differ in reliability and information 
content (airflow, thoracic effort, abdominal effort) [37], and 
a single effort signal can already support respiratory event 
scoring [38]. 
2. Resampling. Every signal is resampled to a modality-
specific common rate after anti-aliasing filtering, so all 
windows of a given duration have identical lengths in 
samples. The target sampling rate for each modality will be 
determined from the dataset audit and reported before model 
development; ECG and respiratory signals are not required to 
share the same rate. 

3. Label unification. Event annotations are converted into a 
binary label (apnea vs. normal). Datasets with per-minute 
annotations propagate the label to the windows inside that 
minute. Datasets with event-level annotations use an overlap 
rule (Section 3.4.4). The difference in temporal precision 
between 
per-minute 
and 
event-level 
annotations 
is 
acknowledged and will be reported as a potential source of 
label noise. 
4. Subject tracking. Each window keeps a subject identifier 
and a dataset identifier for subject-level partitioning. Only de-
identified public data are used, which respects the privacy 
concerns raised for health data in machine learning [21]. 
 
Training on unified multi-dataset data is expected to reduce 
dataset-specific bias, which was shown to cause performance 
degradation on unseen data [27], and heterogeneous multi-
center data support more general models [23], [24]. 
3.4.3 Signal Preprocessing 
Three preprocessing operations are applied and 
later examined by ablation. 
 
- Filtering. ECG is band-pass filtered, with a notch filter 
where power-line interference is present, to suppress baseline 
wander, muscle noise, and mains interference [28], [7], [12]. 
Respiratory signals are low-pass filtered and detrended. 
Alternative decomposition-based spectral denoising exists, 
such as EEMD-ICA [39], but fixed digital filters are preferred 
here for their low computational cost. The specific filter cut-
offs and notch frequency will be determined during the 
dataset audit and preliminary experiments, and reported in the 
final implementation.

3.4.4 Reduced Temporal Windowing 
Signals are segmented into fixed-length windows of 
L{10s,30s}, where the 10-second configuration is the main 
reduced-window setting and the 30-second configuration is 
the longer comparison. Longer contexts such as 3-minute 
inputs are known to help [1], [30], but they increase input 
size, memory, and latency. The reduced-window design tests 
whether short windows, as used in [10] and [14], are 
sufficient when two modalities are available. 
 
For training, windows are extracted with overlap, since 
overlapping slicing combined with sample weighting gave 
the best segment-level detection in [40]. For validation and 
testing, windows do not overlap, so every portion of a 
recording is evaluated once. This asymmetry is applied 
consistently across all configurations and is intended to 
increase training-sample diversity while keeping evaluation 
deterministic and comparable. When event-level annotations
