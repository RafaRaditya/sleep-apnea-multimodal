I. INTRODUCTION 
Sleep apnea is a sleep disorder characterized by repeated 
pauses or reductions in airflow during sleep, potentially 
disrupting breathing patterns and sleep quality. Its detection 
can utilize physiological signals, including electrocardiogram 
(ECG) and respiratory signals. ECG captures cardiac 
electrical activity that may reflect physiological changes 
associated with apnea, while respiratory signals provide 
information about breathing patterns. Previous studies have 
explored deep learning approaches for obstructive sleep apnea 
detection using ECG [1], while incorporating respiratory 
effort has been shown to improve ECG-based assessment of 
sleep-disordered breathing [2]. These findings highlight the 
potential of combining ECG and respiratory signals for 
multimodal sleep apnea detection. 
However, developing sleep apnea detection models across 
multiple datasets presents challenges due to differences in 
sampling rates, signal formats, recording quality, annotation 
structures, and channel availability. These inconsistencies 
may 
affect 
model 
training 
and 
evaluation. 
Signal 
preprocessing, including filtering, amplitude normalization, 
and clipping, may also influence the physiological 
information learned by the model. Furthermore, temporal 
window duration is an important consideration. A 30-second 
window preserves longer temporal context, whereas a 10-
second window enables analysis of shorter segments. 
However, reducing window duration may alter the 
information available for classification, requiring systematic 
evaluation of its impact on detection performance. 
Previous studies have investigated CNN-Transformer 
architectures for ECG-based sleep apnea detection [1], 
multichannel fusion networks [3], and multimodal multiscale 
Transformer models for sleep apnea detection and severity 
assessment [4]. Research has also explored the role of 
temporal context in ECG-based detection [5]. Building on 
these approaches, this study focuses on the combined 
evaluation of multi-dataset harmonization, preprocessing 
strategies, and reduced temporal windowing within a 
multimodal framework. Specifically, a Multimodal Cross-
Attention Transformer will be evaluated for integrating ECG 
and respiratory signals and compared with single-modality 
models and simple fusion approaches. 
This study aims to develop and evaluate a multimodal 
sleep apnea detection model using ECG and respiratory 
signals through multi-dataset harmonization and reduced 
temporal 
windowing. 
The 
UCDDB, 
MIT-BIH 
Polysomnographic, and Apnea-ECG datasets will be assessed 
for signal and annotation availability before harmonization. 
The experiments will examine preprocessing strategies, 
compare 30-second and 10-second windows, and evaluate the 
proposed model against ECG-only, respiratory-only, and 
simple fusion baselines. Performance will be measured using 
accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and area 
under the curve (AUC). The expected contribution is an 
empirical assessment of how dataset harmonization, 
preprocessing, temporal window duration, and multimodal 
integration influence sleep apnea detection performance. 
II. RELATED WORK 
A. ECG-Based Sleep Apnea Detection 
Electrocardiography (ECG) has been investigated as a 
source of physiological information for automated sleep apnea 
detection. Liu et al. proposed a CNN-Transformer architecture 
using single-channel ECG signals and incorporated adjacent 
temporal epochs into the input representation [1]. Thompson 
et al. investigated a one-dimensional convolutional neural 
network using features extracted from segmented ECG time 
series [6]. Other studies have examined ECG-based detection 
using electrocardiographic signals [7], deep learning 
classification [8], hybrid CNN-BiLSTM architectures [9], and 
end-to-end learning from raw ECG recordings [10]. 
Machine learning and deep learning approaches have also 
been investigated using single-lead ECG and ECG-derived 
features. Islam et al. explored sleep apnea detection through 
HRV and SpO2-related analysis of single-lead ECG signals 
[11], while Kumar et al. investigated a 1D-CNN-based 
approach [12]. Duan et al. proposed a multi-context-scale 
CNN-LSTM method combined with contrastive learning for 
ECG-based sleep apnea detection [13]. Collectively, these 
studies establish a foundation for automated ECG-based 
detection. However, the use of ECG alone may limit the direct 
availability 
of 
respiratory 
information, 
motivating 
investigation of complementary physiological signals. 
B. Multimodal Physiological Signal FusioN 
Integrating complementary physiological signals is one 
strategy for incorporating information that may not be fully 
represented by a single modality. Xie et al. investigated the 
use of respiratory effort to improve an ECG-based deep 
learning algorithm for assessing sleep-disordered breathing 
[2]. Zakizadeh Irdmousa et al. proposed ApneaTraNet, a deep 
fusion model for sleep apnea-hypopnea detection using ECG 
signals [14]. Fayyaz et al. examined multimodal sleep apnea 
detection under missing or noisy modality conditions [15]. 
These studies motivate further investigation of multimodal 

learning while highlighting the importance of considering the 
characteristics and availability of the input signals. 
Several 
studies 
have 
explored 
different 
fusion 
architectures. Jiao et al. investigated deep attention networks 
with multi-temporal information fusion for sleep apnea 
detection [16]. Wang et al. proposed heterogeneous graph 
fusion for automated obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea 
syndrome diagnosis [17], while Lv et al. developed a 
multichannel fusion network for sleep apnea syndrome 
detection [3]. Hemrajani et al. investigated integrating ECG, 
nasal airflow, and abdominal respiratory effort through 
SleepNet [18]. Zhang et al. proposed a multimodal signal-
fusion multiscale Transformer model for sleep apnea 
detection and severity assessment [4]. Zhu et al. examined the 
fusion of ECG and biometric data for obstructive sleep apnea-
hypopnea syndrome detection [19]. 
Related work also includes multimodal representation 
learning for broader sleep analysis. Lee et al. proposed a 
multimodal hybrid self-supervised learning framework using 
polysomnography signals [20]. ElKomy et al. studied 
differential privacy in several neural network architectures for 
heartbeat detection from multimodal data [21]. Lin et al. 
investigated temporal fusion of ECG and PPG for continuous 
respiratory rate monitoring [22]. Although these studies differ 
in their tasks and input modalities, they provide 
methodological 
context 
for 
learning 
from 
multiple 
physiological data sources. 
The present study investigates cross-attention for 
integrating ECG and respiratory representations. Cross-
attention allows one modality’s representation to incorporate 
information from another modality. Its potential benefit for 
sleep apnea detection must be evaluated against appropriate 
fusion baselines; it should not be assumed to outperform other 
fusion strategies without experimental evidence. 
C. Multi-Dataset Harmonization and Preprocessing 
Training across multiple datasets can expose a model to a 
broader range of physiological recordings, but differences in 
acquisition 
protocols, 
sampling 
frequencies, 
channel 
configurations, 
signal 
distributions, 
and 
annotation 
conventions may complicate model development. Grieger et 
al. introduced AnySleep, a channel-agnostic deep learning 
system for high-resolution sleep staging in multicenter cohorts 
[23]. Penzel discussed the research value of recorded sleep 
data for improving the understanding of sleep [24], while 
Ehrlich et al. described SleepPhases as a workflow framework 
for sleep data harmonization, analysis, and machine learning 
[25]. 
Physiological data harmonization has also been studied in 
other clinical contexts. Moberg et al. discussed challenges and 
potential directions for harmonizing physiological data in 
neurocritical care [26]. Kim and Shin investigated unified 
multi-dataset training for ECG classification generalization 
[27]. These studies provide relevant context for addressing 
dataset heterogeneity, although their findings should not be 
interpreted as direct evidence that harmonization alone 
improves sleep apnea detection. 
Preprocessing is another important consideration in 
physiological signal analysis. Jia et al. reviewed preprocessing 
and denoising techniques for ECG and magnetocardiography 
signals [28]. Ancillon et al. reviewed machine learning 
approaches for anxiety detection using biosignals, providing 
broader context for physiological signal processing and 
classification [29]. The appropriate preprocessing operations 
may depend on the signal characteristics and the task; 
therefore, their effects should be assessed within the intended 
experimental setting. 
In this study, ECG and respiratory signals are processed 
using a common harmonization pipeline before model 
training. Filtering, normalization, and amplitude clipping are 
treated as separate components for evaluation. This enables 
the experiments to examine whether each operation 
contributes to performance, rather than attributing any 
observed improvement to the entire preprocessing pipeline 
without further analysis. 
D. Temporal Windowing for Sleep Apnea Detection 
Temporal windowing determines how much signal 
information is provided to a model for each prediction. The 
choice of window duration can affect the temporal context 
available to the model and the amount of data processed per 
prediction. Previous work has examined temporal context in 
ECG-based sleep apnea detection [1], [13]. Yamane et al. 
investigated the use of appropriate time windows for single-
lead ECG-based screening, considering ECG information 
before and after the target apnea occurrence [30]. Ryu et al. 
investigated multi-window temporal context under limited 
apnea-label availability [5]. 
These studies support the importance of evaluating 
temporal context, but they do not establish a universal optimal 
window duration for every dataset, model, or prediction task. 
Shorter windows may reduce the amount of input data 
processed for each prediction, but may also provide less 
temporal information. Longer windows provide more context 
but increase the input duration. The practical trade-off 
depends on the experimental setup and the target application. 
Accordingly, this study compares 10-second and 30-
second windows within a multimodal ECG-respiratory 
detection pipeline. Both configurations should be evaluated 
using consistent data partitions, preprocessing procedures, 
model settings, and performance metrics. The resulting 
comparison can then establish whether window duration 
influences detection performance in the proposed setting. 
E. Research Gap and Positioning of the Proposed Study 
The reviewed literature covers ECG-based sleep apnea 
detection, 
multimodal 
fusion, 
physiological 
data 
harmonization, preprocessing, and temporal modeling. Based 
on this literature, the proposed study focuses on four 
methodological aspects. 
First, multi-dataset harmonization of ECG and respiratory 
signals is investigated because datasets can differ in recording 
conditions, 
signal 
representations, 
and 
annotation 
conventions. Prior studies have addressed sleep-data 
workflows, physiological-data harmonization, and unified 
multi-dataset ECG training [23], [25]–[27]. The present study 
focuses on applying harmonization to ECG and respiratory 
inputs within a common sleep apnea detection pipeline. 
Second, reduced temporal windowing is investigated 
through a direct comparison of 10-second and 30-second 
windows. Previous studies have explored temporal context 
and window selection in ECG-based sleep apnea detection [1], 
[13], [30], [5]. However, the relevance of these durations to 
the proposed multimodal pipeline must be established 

experimentally rather than inferred from studies using 
different temporal configurations. 
Third, cross-attention-based ECG-respiratory fusion is 
evaluated as a strategy for learning interactions between 
modalities. Existing work has explored attention-based fusion, 
multichannel networks, heterogeneous graph fusion, and 
multimodal Transformer architectures [16]–[19]. The 
proposed contribution is therefore an empirical evaluation of 
cross-attention 
in 
combination 
with 
the 
selected 
harmonization and windowing strategy, rather than a claim 
that multimodal fusion itself has not been studied. 
Fourth, the individual contribution of preprocessing 
components is examined through ablation experiments. Prior 
research has reviewed physiological signal preprocessing and 
denoising techniques [28]. This study evaluates filtering, 
normalization, and amplitude clipping separately, where 
feasible, to determine how each operation affects detection 
performance. 
In summary, the proposed study brings together multi-
dataset harmonization, cross-attention-based ECG-respiratory 
fusion, a comparison of 10-second and 30-second temporal 
windows, and preprocessing ablation within one experimental 
framework. Controlled experiments will be used to evaluate 
the individual and combined contributions of these design 
choices to multimodal sleep apnea detection. 
 
 
III. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY 
3.1 Research Design 
  This study adopts a quantitative experimental research 
design to develop and evaluate a multimodal sleep apnea 
detection model using electrocardiogram (ECG) and 
respiratory signals. The experimental approach is selected to 
systematically investigate how multi-dataset harmonization, 
preprocessing strategies, temporal window duration, and 
multimodal fusion affect detection performance. Rather than 
assuming that a particular configuration is superior, 
controlled experiments are used to empirically assess the 
contribution of each methodological component. The design 
follows the logic of recent ECG-based apnea studies, which 
compare model variants under identical data partitions and 
report the effect of individual design choices [1], [5], [9]. 
 
  The research is structured as a comparative experimental 
study combined with ablation experiments, organized into 
four experimental groups. Each experiment is guided by a 
specific research question, as summarized below. 
 
Experiment 1: modality and fusion comparison. The 
proposed Multimodal Cross-Attention Transformer is 
compared against ECG-only, respiratory-only, and simple 
fusion baselines. This comparison is motivated by evidence 
that adding respiratory information to ECG improves 
detection. Handcrafted ECG and respiratory features 
outperformed single-signal approaches [31], and adding 
thoracic movement to ECG raised apnea event detection 
accuracy from \(84.1\%\) to \(89.0\%\) [32]. Directly 
measured respiratory effort has also been shown to be 
superior to ECG-derived respiration [2], and combining 
cardiac with audio signals can further improve assessment 
[33]. Several fusion strategies have been reported, including 
attention-based channel fusion [3], graph-based fusion [17], 
multimodal fusion with airflow and abdominal effort [18], 
ECG-SpO2 fusion [4], and ECG-biometric fusion [19]. These 
studies motivate the question of whether cross-attention 
provides a measurable difference over single-modality and 
conventional fusion approaches [16]. 
 
Experiment 2: temporal window duration. The influence of 
temporal context is examined by comparing 10-second and 
30-second segments under identical conditions. Previous 
work shows that adjacent context and window length matter: 
a 3-minute input improved a CNN-Transformer [1], temporal 
context improved F1-scores under limited labels [5], and a 
few minutes before and after an event improved estimation 
[30]. The wavelet-based study in [34] also considered the 
effect of input time window explicitly, and window size 
affected both accuracy and speed in [6]. At the same time, 
short segments of 10 s [10] and 5 s [14] have reported high 
accuracy. This experiment therefore tests whether reduced 
temporal windowing can maintain or improve performance 
while reducing the amount of signal processed per prediction. 
 
Experiment 3: preprocessing ablation. Filtering, 
normalization, and amplitude clipping are removed or added 
one at a time to determine their individual contributions. This 
is relevant because raw single-lead ECG with minimal 
preprocessing can already achieve strong results [35], while 
respiratory and oximetry pipelines usually rely on careful 
signal conditioning [36]. ECG denoising is also known to be 
an important, noise-type-dependent step [28]. 
 
Experiment 
4:

standardized access across sleep repositories limits 
reproducibility and multi-dataset research [25], and

prerequisite for robust machine learning [26]. These 
investigations are informed by previous studies on 
physiological data harmonization and preprocessing [23]–

To operationalize Experiment 4, the harmonization 
comparison will involve the following training and 
evaluation configurations, subject to verification of signal 
availability and annotation compatibility during the dataset 
audit: (i) single-dataset training evaluated on held-out 
subjects from the same dataset; (ii) combined-dataset training 
evaluated on held-out subjects across all datasets; and, where 
dataset compatibility permits, (iii) leave-one-dataset-out 
evaluation, in which one dataset is entirely excluded from 
training and used only for testing, following the logic of [27] 
and [23]. 
 
In terms of variables, the independent variables are the model 
configuration (proposed, ECG-only, respiratory-only, simple 
fusion), window duration (10 s vs. 30 s), and preprocessing 

setting. The dependent variables are accuracy, sensitivity, 
specificity, precision, F1-score, and the area under the 
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Controlled 
variables are the subject-level data partitions, optimizer, 
training budget, batch size, learning-rate tuning rule, labeling 
strategy, evaluation protocol, and random seeds, which are 
held constant across comparisons for a fair assessment. 
Through this design, the study aims to provide an empirical 
evaluation of the combined effects of harmonization, 
preprocessing, temporal window reduction, and cross-
attention fusion on sleep apnea detection. 
 
3.2 Research Flow 
 
The research begins with auditing three candidate datasets: 
UCDDB, MIT-BIH PSG, and Apnea-ECG to examine 
recording 
eligibility, 
subject 
information, 
channel 
availability, and annotations. Subsequently, the experimental 
protocol is defined, including target labels, channel 
configurations, and ablation settings. The data are then 
partitioned at the subject level into training, validation, and 
testing sets to prevent subject overlap. 
 
Next, data harmonization and preprocessing are performed, 
followed by temporal windowing using 10-second and 30-
second configurations with corresponding label assignments. 
The training and validation data are subsequently used to 
train and tune the proposed model and three baseline models. 
During this phase, the models are optimized through weight 
updates, while validation is utilized for setting selections, 
checkpoints, and threshold tuning. For the proposed model, 
this process involves training the ECG and respiratory 
encoder Transformers, the cross-attention fusion mechanism, 
and the pooling and classification layers. Concurrently, the 
baseline models consisting of ECG-only, respiration-only, 
and simple fusion configurations are trained and validated 
under the same conditions. 
 
After the best model for each configuration is selected and 
the configurations are finalized, the models are evaluated on 
the held-out test subjects. These frozen models are used for 
test-set prediction to ensure an unbiased evaluation. Finally, 
classification metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, 
specificity, precision, F1-score, and ROC-AUC, are analyzed 
to compare model performance. Where reference annotations 
support it, an optional event or AHI analysis is also 
performed, leading to the comparative analysis and 
conclusions. 
 
3.3 Dataset 
The datasets used in this study are obtained from PhysioNet, 
a publicly accessible repository of physiological signal 
databases for biomedical research. Three publicly available 
databases are considered: UCDDB (University College 
Dublin Sleep Apnea Database), MIT-BIH Polysomnographic 
Database (MIT-BIH PSG), and Apnea-ECG Database. These 
databases contain physiological recordings relevant to sleep 
apnea research, particularly electrocardiogram (ECG) and 
respiratory-related signals. The combination of these datasets 
is intended to provide a broader source of physiological data 
for developing and evaluating the proposed multimodal sleep 
apnea detection approach. 
 
Because the proposed model requires both ECG and 
respiratory inputs, the availability of these modalities in each 
dataset is not assumed but will be verified through a dataset 
audit. The audit will examine recording eligibility, subject 
information, channel availability, annotation structure, and 
signal quality. The following table serves as an audit template 
and will be completed prior to harmonization and model 
development. 
3.4 Proposed Method 
3.4.1 Method Overview 
The proposed method detects sleep apnea by 
integrating ECG and respiratory signals in a multimodal deep 
learning pipeline. The pipeline has six stages: (1) multi-
dataset harmonization, (2) preprocessing, (3) reduced 
temporal windowing, (4) modality-specific Transformer 
encoding, (5) cross-attention fusion, and (6) pooling and 
window-level classification. Each window is classified as 
sleep apnea or normal. Unlike approaches that convert signals 
into time-frequency images before classification [41], [42], 
the proposed model operates directly on one-dimensional 
signal sequences, which keeps the pipeline simple and avoids 
a separate image-construction step. 
3.4.2  Multi Dataset Harmonization 
1. Channel mapping. ECG and respiratory channels are 
mapped to a unified naming scheme, so each recording 
provides an ECG channel and a respiratory channel. 
Respiratory sources differ in reliability and information 
content (airflow, thoracic effort, abdominal effort) [37], and 
a single effort signal can already support respiratory event 
scoring [38]. 
2. Resampling. Every signal is resampled to a modality-
specific common rate after anti-aliasing filtering, so all 
windows of a given duration have identical lengths in 
samples. The target sampling rate for each modality will be 
determined from the dataset audit and reported before model 
development; ECG and respiratory signals are not required to 
share the same rate. 

3. Label unification. Event annotations are converted into a 
binary label (apnea vs. normal). Datasets with per-minute 
annotations propagate the label to the windows inside that 
minute. Datasets with event-level annotations use an overlap 
rule (Section 3.4.4). The difference in temporal precision 
between 
per-minute 
and 
event-level 
annotations 
is 
acknowledged and will be reported as a potential source of 
label noise. 
4. Subject tracking. Each window keeps a subject identifier 
and a dataset identifier for subject-level partitioning. Only de-
identified public data are used, which respects the privacy 
concerns raised for health data in machine learning [21]. 
 
Training on unified multi-dataset data is expected to reduce 
dataset-specific bias, which was shown to cause performance 
degradation on unseen data [27], and heterogeneous multi-
center data support more general models [23], [24]. 
3.4.3 Signal Preprocessing 
Three preprocessing operations are applied and 
later examined by ablation. 
 
- Filtering. ECG is band-pass filtered, with a notch filter 
where power-line interference is present, to suppress baseline 
wander, muscle noise, and mains interference [28], [7], [12]. 
Respiratory signals are low-pass filtered and detrended. 
Alternative decomposition-based spectral denoising exists, 
such as EEMD-ICA [39], but fixed digital filters are preferred 
here for their low computational cost. The specific filter cut-
offs and notch frequency will be determined during the 
dataset audit and preliminary experiments, and reported in the 
final implementation. 
- Normalization. Each recording is z-score normalized per 
channel,

3.4.4 Reduced Temporal Windowing 
Signals are segmented into fixed-length windows of 
L{10s,30s}, where the 10-second configuration is the main 
reduced-window setting and the 30-second configuration is 
the longer comparison. Longer contexts such as 3-minute 
inputs are known to help [1], [30], but they increase input 
size, memory, and latency. The reduced-window design tests 
whether short windows, as used in [10] and [14], are 
sufficient when two modalities are available. 
 
For training, windows are extracted with overlap, since 
overlapping slicing combined with sample weighting gave 
the best segment-level detection in [40]. For validation and 
testing, windows do not overlap, so every portion of a 
recording is evaluated once. This asymmetry is applied 
consistently across all configurations and is intended to 
increase training-sample diversity while keeping evaluation 
deterministic and comparable. When event-level annotations 
are available, a window is labeled apnea if at least \(50\%\) of 
its duration overlaps an annotated apnea or hypopnea event, 
and normal otherwise. Both configurations use the same 
labeling rule and subject-level splits, so any difference in 
performance is attributable to window length.

The principle is that cardiac fluctuations and respiratory

does not depend on a single branch and stays robust to 
missing or noisy channels, a problem addressed explicitly in 
[15].

  The fused sequences are aggregated by pooling over the 
token dimension into vectors zE and zR. Mean pooling is 
adopted as the primary pooling method for its simplicity and 
low parameter count; attention pooling will be considered 
only as a secondary hyperparameter if preliminary results 
indicate that it materially improves validation performance. 
The pooled vectors are concatenated and passed to a 
classification head (dropout, a hidden fully connected layer

3.4.8 Baseline Models 
  Three baselines share the same encoder design, training 
budget, and splits: 
 
1. ECG-only: the ECG Transformer encoder with pooling and 
classifier. 
2. Respiration-only: the respiratory Transformer encoder 
with pooling and classifier. 
3. Simple fusion: both encoders with plain concatenation of 
the pooled features and no cross-attention. 
 
  Differences between the proposed model and the simple 
fusion baseline therefore isolate the effect of cross-attention. 
Classical references such as SVM, random forest, and LSTM 
[11] are noted in the related work, but are not part of the 
controlled comparison.

validation set, and reported in the final implementation. 
Hyperparameters (patch size, d, number of layers and heads, 
dropout, learning rate) are tuned using only the validation set, 
and the decision threshold is selected on the validation set 
(e.g., maximizing F1-score) before being frozen.

measures 
overall 
correctness, 
sensitivity 
measures the ability to identify apnea windows, and 
specificity measures the ability to identify normal windows. 
The F1-score balances precision and sensitivity, which 
matters because apnea windows are typically fewer than 
normal windows. AUC evaluates discriminative ability 
independently of a specific cutoff. These metrics are the 
standard set in ECG-based apnea detection [9], [35], [40], 
[43]. 
 
3.5.2 Validation Protocol 
  1. Subject-level partitioning. Data are divided into training, 
validation, and test sets at the subject level, so no subject 
appears in more than one set. Windows from the same 
recording are highly correlated, and window-level splits 
would inflate results. Participant-grouped cross-validation is 
used in [5] for the same reason. 
  2. Strict role separation. The training set updates weights, 
the validation set is used for model selection, checkpointing, 
and threshold tuning, and the test set is used once. Models 
and thresholds are frozen before test prediction to prevent 
data leakage and optimistic bias. 
  3. Identical partitions across experiments. The same subject-
level partitions are used for all comparative and ablation 
experiments, so differences come from the configuration 
being tested. 
  4. Repeated runs. Each configuration is trained with several 
random seeds, and results are reported as mean ± standard 
deviation. Confidence intervals for the metrics are estimated 
by bootstrap resampling at the subject level, so that the 
resampling unit matches the subject-level design. 

  5. Comparison of configurations. The proposed model is 
compared with the three baselines (Experiment 1), 10-second 
with 30-second windows (Experiment 2), and each 
preprocessing variant with the full pipeline (Experiment 3). 
  6. Generalization across datasets. Where the datasets allow, 
a leave-one-dataset-out evaluation can be added to assess 
generalization under distribution shift, following the logic of 
[27] and [23]. This is treated as a separate evaluation protocol 
from the subject-level split and is reported separately. 
 
 
3.5.3 Computational Efficiency Analysis 
  Because reduced temporal windowing is one of the 
contributions of this study, efficiency is evaluated alongside 
detection performance. Parameter count and inference time 
per window are reported for the proposed model and the 
baselines under both the 10-second and 30-second 
configurations. This analysis quantifies whether the reduced 
window provides a meaningful reduction in computational 
cost at comparable detection performance. 
 
3.5.4 Event Level and AHI Analysis 
  Where reference annotations support it, window predictions 
are aggregated per recording to estimate the apnea-hypopnea 
index (AHI). Agreement with the reference AHI is measured 
with Spearman's correlation, mean absolute error, and a 
Bland-Altman plot [45]. Recordings are then assigned 
severity classes by AHI thresholds, and agreement is 
quantified with accuracy and Cohen's kappa, as in the clinical 
validation of [43] and in [2]. This analysis complements the 
window-level evaluation with a more clinically relevant view 
of performance. It is treated as an optional secondary analysis 
and is not used as the primary criterion for model selection, 
because AHI estimation requires sleep-duration information 
and an aggregation procedure that must be validated 
separately from window-level classification.
